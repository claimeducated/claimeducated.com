How to Vet a Contractor After Storm Damage (Before You Sign)

The first contractor to knock after a storm may not be the right one for your repairs. Here is what to watch for, what to verify, and what to never sign without reading first.

7 min read  ·  July 12, 2026

The storm has passed. Your home has damage. Before the adjuster has even scheduled an inspection, someone is knocking on your door offering to fix it.

This is not a coincidence. After every major storm or hurricane, contractors follow the damage trail, moving from neighborhood to neighborhood, sometimes from state to state, looking for homeowners who are stressed, overwhelmed, and ready to sign something quickly just to feel like progress is being made.

Some of those contractors are perfectly legitimate. Others are not. The problem is that in the days following a disaster, it is difficult to tell the difference, and the decisions you make in that window can affect both the quality of your repairs and the outcome of your insurance claim.

Here is what to know before you open the door, and before you sign anything.

What Is a Storm Chaser Contractor?

Storm chasing is a business model, not a compliment. A storm chaser is a contractor, often a roofing company, who monitors weather events and disaster declarations and then dispatches crews to affected areas specifically to solicit repair work from homeowners. They may travel hundreds of miles to reach a storm-affected region, work the area intensively for weeks, and then move on to the next event.

The speed of their arrival is part of the pitch. They present themselves as ready to start immediately when local contractors may have longer backlogs. That urgency can feel like a benefit when your roof is damaged and rain is in the forecast. It can also be a pressure tactic designed to get you to sign before you have had time to think.

Not every out-of-area contractor is a bad actor. After a major regional disaster, local contractor capacity often cannot meet the volume of repair needs, and bringing in crews from elsewhere is sometimes necessary. The distinction is not where the contractor is from. It is how they conduct business and whether they hold up under basic verification.

Red Flags to Recognize Before You Engage

Certain behaviors are consistent warning signs regardless of how polished the pitch sounds.

Unsolicited Door-to-Door Solicitation

A contractor who shows up at your door without being called, particularly in the days immediately following a storm, is worth approaching with caution. This does not automatically disqualify them, but it means verification matters more, not less. Never allow a contractor you did not contact to begin work, access your roof, or take photographs of your property without first confirming who they are and who they work for.

Pressure to Sign Immediately

Any contractor who insists you must sign today, tells you the offer expires tonight, or discourages you from getting other estimates is using pressure tactics. A reputable contractor will give you time to review a contract, ask questions, and compare options. Pressure to decide quickly is a signal to slow down, not speed up.

Offering to Waive Your Deductible

If a contractor offers to cover your deductible, absorb it, or build it into the work so you do not have to pay it out of pocket, walk away. In most states this practice is illegal. It is also frequently used as a hook to inflate the total claim estimate to cover the deductible amount, which can constitute insurance fraud. Your deductible is your financial responsibility under your policy. A contractor who offers to make it disappear is not doing you a favor.

Requesting Full Payment Upfront

A standard contractor payment structure involves a deposit at contract signing, a progress payment at an agreed milestone, and a final payment upon satisfactory completion. Demanding full payment before work begins is a red flag. It removes your leverage if the work is incomplete or substandard, and it is a common pattern in contractor fraud following disasters.

No Verifiable Local Presence or History

A contractor with no local address, no verifiable reviews, no established web presence, and no references who cannot be reached is a contractor you cannot vet. If a business cannot be found independently outside of the materials they hand you at your door, that absence of information is itself a warning.

Vague or Incomplete Contracts

Any contract that does not specify the full scope of work, the materials to be used, the timeline, the payment schedule, and the warranty terms is a contract you should not sign. Vague contracts protect the contractor, not you. If the written document does not match what you were told verbally, the written document is what governs.

What to Verify Before Signing Anything

Verification takes time. That is the point. Any contractor who cannot or will not give you time to verify their credentials before signing is telling you something important about how they operate.

Proof of Insurance

Request a certificate of insurance that shows current general liability coverage and workers' compensation coverage. Both matter. General liability covers damage to your property if something goes wrong during the repair. Workers' compensation covers injuries to workers on your property. Without workers' comp, an injured worker on your roof could become your financial liability.

Do not accept a verbal assurance that they are insured. Request the actual certificate and verify that the coverage dates are current. You can call the insurance company listed on the certificate to confirm the policy is active.

Licensing

Licensing requirements for contractors vary significantly by state. In Texas, for example, there is no state-level general contractor license requirement and no state licensing board for general contractors. This means that in Texas, licensing alone cannot be your verification standard. Focus instead on insurance verification, online reviews, and business history.

If you are in a state with contractor licensing requirements, ask for the license number and verify it through your state's licensing board before signing.

Reviews and Business History

Search the contractor's business name on Google, the Better Business Bureau, and any other review platforms where they appear. Look for patterns, not just star ratings. A business with a handful of five-star reviews and nothing else, or reviews that are all recent and clustered after a storm event, warrants more scrutiny than one with an established review history over time.

Ask for references from past customers in your area and follow up with them. Ask specifically about the quality of the finished work, whether the project stayed on budget, and how the contractor handled any issues that came up.

A Physical Business Address

Confirm the contractor has a verifiable physical business address, not just a phone number and a truck. A business operating without a fixed address has no accountability trail if problems arise after the job is done and they have moved on.

Assignment of Benefits: Read This Before You Sign

One of the most consequential things a storm-soliciting contractor may ask you to sign is an assignment of benefits, sometimes called an AOB. This is a contract provision that legally transfers your insurance claim rights to the contractor. Once signed, the contractor can contact your insurer directly, negotiate on the claim, and receive insurance payments in your name without your ongoing involvement or approval.

AOB agreements have been associated with inflated estimates, disputes between contractors and insurers, and situations where the homeowner loses visibility into and control over their own claim. Some states have enacted legislation restricting AOB use in part because of the problems they have created in post-disaster claims environments.

Before signing any document that includes an AOB clause, understand exactly what you are agreeing to. If you are unsure, do not sign until you have had time to read it carefully or consult with someone who can help you understand it.

How Contractor Choices Affect Your Claim

Your choice of contractor is directly connected to your insurance claim in ways that are not always obvious at the outset.

The estimate your contractor provides may be used to support a supplement request if the insurer's estimate is lower than the actual cost of repairs. A contractor who is familiar with the claims process, understands how to document scope of work, and can produce a line-item estimate in a format your insurer can work with is a meaningful asset in that process.

Conversely, a contractor who inflates estimates, uses non-standard materials without disclosure, or produces documentation that does not align with the actual damage can create problems with your claim that take significant time and effort to resolve.

If your insurer's estimate and your contractor's estimate differ significantly, the supplement process is the appropriate path. Our article How to Request a Supplement on Your Insurance Claim explains how to approach that conversation effectively.

Taking Your Time Is Not Delay, It Is Protection

After a storm, the pressure to act quickly is real. If your roof is open to the elements or your windows are broken, getting repairs started matters. But there is a difference between taking reasonable temporary protective measures, which are appropriate and should be documented, and rushing into a permanent repair contract with a contractor you have not vetted.

Temporary tarping, board-up, and emergency water mitigation can happen quickly through established local services while you take the time to properly evaluate contractors for permanent repairs. Most policies require you to prevent further damage, but they do not require you to sign a permanent repair contract within 48 hours of the storm.

Final Thought

The contractor who arrives first is not necessarily the contractor you should hire. In the aftermath of a storm, taking 24 to 48 hours to verify credentials, review contracts carefully, and compare at least two or three estimates is not caution that slows your recovery. It is the step that protects it.

Your home and your insurance claim are both significant assets. The contractor you choose affects both.

A contractor who pressures you to sign before you are ready is showing you exactly how they will handle the rest of the job.
