What Is a CAT Adjuster? What to Expect After a Major Disaster

After a major hurricane or disaster, your claim may be handled by someone you have never heard of before: a catastrophe adjuster. Understanding who they are and how they work changes how you prepare.

6 min read  ·  July 12, 2026

You filed your claim. Now you are waiting to hear about an inspection. But instead of your regular insurance company contact, someone new reaches out: a CAT adjuster.

If you have never heard that term before, you are not alone. Most policyholders only encounter a catastrophe adjuster after a major storm, hurricane, or widespread disaster event, and usually at the worst possible moment: when their home has just been damaged and the claims process is only beginning.

Here is what a CAT adjuster is, how the catastrophe claims process differs from a standard claim, and what you can do right now to be prepared for the inspection.

What Is a CAT Adjuster?

CAT is short for catastrophe. A CAT adjuster is an insurance adjuster deployed specifically to handle claims that result from a large-scale disaster event, such as a hurricane, tornado outbreak, major hailstorm, wildfire, or flooding. These events generate claim volume that far exceeds what a carrier's local staff can handle, so insurers bring in additional adjusters to manage the surge.

CAT adjusters come from two primary sources. Some are staff adjusters, employed directly by your insurance company, who are dispatched from other regions when a catastrophe strikes. Others are independent adjusters, meaning they are contractors who work for independent adjusting firms and are hired by insurers on a temporary basis to handle claim volume during a CAT event.

In either case, the CAT adjuster assigned to your claim is acting on behalf of your insurance company. They are not a neutral third party. Their role is to inspect the damage, document it, and produce an estimate of covered losses based on your policy terms.

How a CAT Claim Differs From a Standard Claim

In a routine claim, you may work with a locally based adjuster who is familiar with your region, local construction costs, and local building codes. The process may feel more measured because the claim volume is manageable and timelines are more predictable.

A catastrophe claim operates under different conditions, and understanding those conditions helps you navigate the process more effectively.

High Volume, Time Pressure

After a major disaster, a single CAT adjuster may be managing dozens of claims simultaneously. This creates real time pressure on every inspection. An adjuster working through a high volume of claims in a compressed timeframe may spend less time on each property than a standard claim inspection would allow.

This is not a reason for alarm, but it is a reason to be prepared. A thorough inspection depends in part on you being ready to guide the adjuster through every area of damage rather than assuming they will find everything independently.

Unfamiliarity With Local Conditions

CAT adjusters are often brought in from other states or regions. They may not be familiar with local construction costs, local contractor pricing, or the specific building codes and requirements in your area. Xactimate, the estimating software used by most adjusters, uses regional pricing databases, but local market conditions, particularly after a widespread disaster when contractor demand spikes, may not be fully reflected in the initial estimate.

This is one reason why obtaining independent contractor estimates after a CAT event is important. If your contractor's pricing differs significantly from the insurer's estimate, that gap is documentable and can be the basis for a supplement request.

Longer Timelines

Catastrophe claims generally take longer to resolve than standard claims. High claim volume, contractor availability, material lead times, and the complexity of widespread damage all contribute to extended timelines. Understanding this at the outset helps you plan for the recovery period and make informed decisions about temporary housing, temporary repairs, and contractor scheduling.

Your policy's Coverage D, also called loss of use or additional living expenses coverage, is specifically designed for situations where your home is uninhabitable during the repair process. Review your declarations page to understand your limit and how it is structured. If you need a refresher on how to read that document, our article How to Read Your Home Insurance Declarations Page walks through every section.

What to Do Before the CAT Adjuster Arrives

The work you do before the inspection directly affects the outcome of the inspection. Here is what to prioritize.

Document Everything Before Any Cleanup

Photograph and video every area of damage before any debris removal, temporary repairs, or cleanup begins. Walk every room. Document the exterior from multiple angles. Capture damage to the roof, siding, windows, gutters, fencing, outbuildings, and any vehicles or personal property affected. Go into attic spaces if it is safe to do so.

The adjuster's inspection happens once. Your documentation is the record that supports your claim if questions arise later. Our article How to Document Property Damage for Your Insurance Claim covers exactly what to capture and how to organize it.

Make Temporary Repairs to Prevent Further Damage

Most policies require policyholders to take reasonable steps to prevent additional damage after a loss. Tarping a damaged roof, boarding broken windows, and addressing active water intrusion are appropriate protective measures. Document these temporary repairs with photographs and keep all receipts, as these costs may be reimbursable under your policy.

Do not make permanent repairs before the adjuster has inspected the damage. Permanent repairs completed before the inspection can complicate your claim by making it difficult to document the original scope of loss.

Make a Written Damage List

Before the adjuster arrives, write out every area and item you believe was damaged. Organize it by location: exterior, roof, each interior room, outbuildings, personal property. This list serves two purposes. It helps you stay organized during the inspection walkthrough, and it gives you a reference point to compare against the estimate when it arrives.

Have Your Policy Documents Ready

Locate your declarations page and have your policy number and claim number on hand. Know your coverage limits, your deductible, and whether you have any endorsements that may be relevant to the loss. If you do not have this information memorized, your declarations page is the place to find it.

During the Inspection: How to Participate Effectively

You have the right to be present during the adjuster's inspection of your property. Being present is not just acceptable, it is advisable.

Walk through the property with the adjuster. Point out every area of damage, including areas that may not be immediately visible such as attic damage, interior water staining, damage beneath flooring, or damage to systems like HVAC or electrical that may have been affected. Do not assume the adjuster will find everything without prompting.

Ask questions during the walkthrough. If the adjuster notes something or moves past an area quickly, it is reasonable to ask what they observed and whether it will appear in the estimate. Take your own notes during the inspection and photograph anything the adjuster spends significant time reviewing.

Be specific about damage that concerns you. If you believe there is structural damage, hidden moisture, or damage beneath a surface that is not yet visible, say so. The adjuster can note it for follow-up even if it cannot be confirmed during the initial visit.

After the Inspection: Reviewing the Estimate

Once the estimate arrives, read it carefully before accepting any payment or signing any documents. Compare the scope of loss against your own damage list and documentation. Look for rooms or items that are missing, quantities that appear too low, or line items that seem inconsistent with the actual damage.

Given the time pressure CAT adjusters work under and the volume of claims they manage simultaneously, initial catastrophe estimates sometimes miss items or underestimate scope. This is not always intentional, but it is common enough that a careful review is always worth doing.

If items are missing or underestimated, a supplement request is the appropriate path. A supplement is a documented request to your insurer for additional coverage based on damage not captured in the original estimate. Our article How to Request a Supplement on Your Insurance Claim explains how to approach that process effectively.

For a more detailed breakdown of how to read the estimate itself, including what each column means and what to look for, see our article How to Read Your Insurance Claim Estimate Line by Line.

One More Thing: Watch for Contractor Solicitation After a CAT Event

After a major disaster, contractors appear quickly. Some are legitimate local professionals who can help you recover. Others are storm chasers who follow catastrophe events and may not have your best interests in mind. Knowing how to identify the difference before you sign anything protects both your claim and your home.

Our article How to Vet a Contractor After Storm Damage covers the red flags to watch for and what to verify before any contract is signed.

Final Thought

A CAT adjuster is not your adversary, but they are also not your advocate. They are a professional working under significant volume and time pressure to document and estimate your loss based on what they observe and what your policy covers. The more prepared you are going into that inspection, the more complete that documentation is likely to be.

After a disaster, it is easy to feel overwhelmed by the process. Breaking it into steps, starting with your documentation before the adjuster arrives, helps you stay in control of the information that determines your outcome.

The inspection is one moment in time. Your preparation for it can last for weeks afterward.
