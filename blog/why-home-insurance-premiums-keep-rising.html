Why Your Home Insurance Premium Keeps Going Up

Premiums have climbed more than 46% since 2021. Here is what is actually driving the increases, and what it could mean for your claim payout if damage happens today.

6 min read  ·  June 16, 2026

You open the renewal notice. The premium went up again. No claims. No changes to your home. Just a higher bill.

This is happening to homeowners across the country. According to data from Insurify, average home insurance premiums have climbed more than 46% since 2021, rising roughly three times faster than general inflation over the same period. In 2025 alone, the average annual premium jumped 12%.

But here is what many homeowners are not thinking about: the same forces pushing premiums higher are also reshaping what happens during a claim. Understanding the connection between rising premiums and claim outcomes is information every policyholder needs right now.

What Is Actually Driving Premium Increases

Several factors are converging at once. They are not temporary, and most are not within a homeowner's control.

Rebuild Costs Have Risen Sharply

Between 2020 and 2022, the cost to replace a home surged approximately 55%, driven by post-pandemic inflation and supply chain disruptions. Those costs have not fully come back down. Labor, materials, and skilled trades all cost more than they did just a few years ago.

When it becomes more expensive to rebuild a home after a loss, insurers pay out more on claims. That increase in claim costs flows directly into premium pricing.

Tariffs Are Adding Pressure

Since April 2025, a 10% universal tariff has been applied to most imported goods, with steeper tariffs targeting lumber, steel, and aluminum specifically. These are core building materials. According to Insurify's projections, tariffs are expected to add roughly $106 to the average homeowner's annual insurance cost. More importantly, they raise the price of actually repairing your home after damage, which affects what those repairs cost at claim time.

Severe Weather Is Increasing

The National Centers for Environmental Information recorded 60 natural disasters over the past three years that each caused more than $1 billion in damage. Adjusted for inflation, that averages out to approximately $149 billion in losses per year. More frequent and more severe weather events mean more claims, which puts upward pressure on rates across entire regions, even for homeowners who have never filed a claim.

Reinsurance Costs Have Increased

Insurance companies purchase their own insurance, called reinsurance, to protect against catastrophic losses. When reinsurers raise their prices due to elevated losses, primary insurers pass those costs to policyholders through higher premiums.

Labor Shortages

The construction industry is estimated to need nearly 350,000 additional workers to meet current demand. Fewer skilled workers means repairs take longer and cost more, which affects both the speed of your claim resolution and the final repair costs.

What This Means for Your Claim

Here is the part most renewal notices do not explain: paying more for your premium does not automatically mean your coverage has kept pace with today's rebuild costs.

Your claim payout is governed by the terms in your policy, specifically your coverage limits, your deductible, and your settlement method. If your dwelling coverage limit has not been adjusted to reflect current construction costs, you could be significantly underinsured at the moment you need coverage most.

The Underinsurance Risk

Consider a home with a dwelling coverage limit set several years ago at $300,000. Given the dramatic rise in material and labor costs since 2021, that same home might cost $400,000 or more to rebuild today. If a total loss occurs, the gap between what insurance pays and what rebuilding actually costs falls on the homeowner.

This is not a hypothetical. Rising material costs are resulting in many homes being left underinsured, sometimes without the homeowner realizing it until a claim is filed.

Your Settlement Method Still Applies

Whether your policy settles claims at Actual Cash Value or Replacement Cost Value matters just as much in a higher-cost environment. If your policy settles at ACV, depreciation will be applied to today's already-higher material costs. If you have replacement cost coverage, understanding how depreciation holdback and the recovery process works is important. You can read more about how these two settlement methods differ in our article Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost: What Is the Difference?

Deductibles and Out-of-Pocket Exposure

Some insurers have responded to elevated risk by increasing deductibles, particularly wind and hail deductibles in storm-prone states. A deductible that felt manageable a few years ago may represent a larger financial burden today, especially when repair costs have also risen. Review your declarations page and confirm what your deductible is for different types of losses before storm season arrives.

What Homeowners Should Do Right Now

Premium increases feel frustrating, especially when you have not filed a claim. But the renewal period is actually a valuable opportunity to review whether your coverage still fits your situation. Here are the steps worth taking:

Check Your Dwelling Coverage Limit

Your Coverage A limit appears on your declarations page. This is the maximum your insurer will pay to rebuild the structure of your home after a covered loss. Compare that number to current construction costs in your area. If your policy was written or last adjusted several years ago, there may be a meaningful gap between your limit and today's rebuild cost. Ask your insurer for a current replacement cost estimate or request an updated review.

Confirm Your Settlement Method

Review your policy's loss settlement provisions. Look for language indicating whether your home and personal property are covered at Replacement Cost Value or Actual Cash Value. If your roof or personal property settles at ACV, understand what depreciation may mean for your payout. If you have questions about what the language in your policy means, the ClaimEducated™ resource library has free guides to help you understand claims terminology.

Review Your Wind and Hail Deductible

Many policies in storm-prone states carry a separate, higher deductible for wind and hail claims. This deductible is often expressed as a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit, not a flat dollar amount. If your Coverage A limit is $350,000 and your wind deductible is 2%, that is $7,000 out of pocket before insurance pays anything on a storm claim. Confirm this number and plan accordingly.

Ask About Inflation Guard or Extended Replacement Cost Endorsements

Some policies include endorsements that automatically adjust your dwelling coverage limit to account for rising construction costs. Others offer extended replacement cost coverage that provides a percentage above your stated limit if rebuild costs exceed it. Ask your insurer whether these options are included in your current policy or available to add.

Document Your Home Now

If damage does happen, the quality of your documentation directly affects the accuracy and efficiency of your claim. A thorough home inventory, photos of every room and all major systems, and records of any upgrades or improvements give you a strong foundation going into a claim. Our free guide, The First 15 Minutes, walks through exactly what to capture and preserve immediately after damage occurs.

A Note on What Higher Premiums Do Not Mean

Paying more for coverage does not mean your insurer will automatically pay more if you file a claim. Your payout is determined by your policy terms, not your premium amount. A higher premium reflects the insurer's increased cost to provide coverage. Whether that coverage is adequate for your specific home at today's costs is a separate question, and one worth asking before a loss, not after.

This is one of the most important things to understand about the current insurance market: the premium environment and the claim environment are related, but they are not the same thing. Understanding both helps you make better decisions about your coverage.

If You Are Already in a Claim

If you are currently navigating a property claim, rising rebuild costs can affect you in a specific way. Contractors may provide estimates that reflect today's material and labor prices, which could exceed what your insurer initially estimates based on older pricing data. This gap is one of the situations where a supplement request may be appropriate.

You can learn more about that process in our article How to Request a Supplement on Your Insurance Claim.

Final Thought

Rising premiums are, at their core, a signal that the cost to repair and replace property has gone up substantially. That same reality applies to your specific home and your specific claim. The homeowners who are best prepared are the ones who understand their coverage limits, know their settlement method, and have documentation ready before damage happens.

A renewal notice is not just a bill. It is a prompt to review whether your coverage still reflects the reality of what it would actually cost to rebuild your home today.

The time to find a coverage gap is before you need to file a claim, not after.
