How to Read Your Home Insurance Declarations Page

Most policyholders have never read this document carefully. It is one page, and it contains nearly everything that determines your claim outcome.

7 min read  ·  June 29, 2026

Every homeowners insurance policy comes with a declarations page. Most people file it away after renewal and never look at it again.

That is a costly habit. The declarations page, often called the dec page, is the single document that summarizes what your policy actually covers, how much it will pay, what you owe out of pocket before coverage applies, and what modifications have been made to your standard policy terms. When damage happens, every number on that page becomes relevant immediately.

Here is what every section means, what to look for, and what to confirm before you ever need to file a claim.

What the Declarations Page Is (and Is Not)

The declarations page is a summary. It is not the full policy. The complete policy includes the policy form, conditions, exclusions, and any endorsements, which together can run dozens of pages. The dec page distills the most important numbers and terms onto one or two pages so you can see your coverage at a glance.

Because it is a summary, it does not explain everything. It tells you what your limits and deductibles are, but the full policy language governs how claims are actually settled. Understanding both matters, and the dec page is the right starting point.

The Basic Information Section

At the top of your declarations page you will typically find identifying information about your policy and property. This includes your policy number, which you will need when reporting a claim. It also includes your policy period, the start and end dates of your current coverage term. If damage occurs outside of that period, coverage may not apply. Confirm the dates are current each time you receive a renewal.

You will also see the named insured, which is the person or persons listed as the policyholder. This matters at claim time because payments and correspondence are generally directed to the named insured. If your property ownership has changed, such as after a marriage or death, confirm the named insured reflects the correct parties.

The insured property address should also appear here. Verify it matches your home exactly, including any unit numbers if applicable.

Coverage A: Dwelling

This is the most important number on the page for most homeowners.

Coverage A is your dwelling coverage. It represents the maximum your insurer will pay to repair or rebuild the physical structure of your home after a covered loss. This includes the walls, roof, floors, built-in appliances, and attached structures such as an attached garage.

A critical and widely misunderstood point: Coverage A is not your home's market value. It is not what you paid for the house. It is what it would cost to rebuild the structure from the ground up using today's materials and labor costs. Those figures are often very different numbers.

Given the significant rise in construction costs over the past several years, Coverage A limits that were set even a few years ago may no longer be adequate. A home that cost $280,000 to rebuild in 2020 may cost $380,000 or more to rebuild today. If your limit has not been updated to reflect that, the gap comes out of your pocket after a major loss.

Review this number carefully and ask your insurer for a current replacement cost estimate if you are uncertain whether your limit is adequate. You can also read more about how your settlement method interacts with this limit in our article Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost: What Is the Difference?

Coverage B: Other Structures

Coverage B covers structures on your property that are detached from the main dwelling. This typically includes a detached garage, fences, sheds, carports, and similar structures.

In many standard policies, Coverage B is set at a percentage of Coverage A, commonly around 10%. On a policy with $300,000 in Coverage A, that would mean $30,000 in Coverage B. If you have a large detached garage, a long fence line, or multiple outbuildings, verify that this limit is sufficient for those structures.

Coverage C: Personal Property

Coverage C covers your personal belongings inside the home: furniture, clothing, electronics, appliances, and similar items. If damage or a covered loss destroys your contents, Coverage C is what applies.

Two things to confirm here. First, check whether your personal property is covered at Actual Cash Value or Replacement Cost Value. Many standard policies default to ACV for personal property, which means depreciation is applied before a payment is made. A five-year-old television that costs $800 to replace today may only pay out $300 at ACV depending on how depreciation is calculated. A replacement cost endorsement for personal property changes that calculation significantly.

Second, be aware that certain high-value items, such as jewelry, firearms, fine art, or collectibles, may have sub-limits under Coverage C that are well below what those items are worth. If you own items of significant value, check whether a scheduled personal property endorsement is in place, or whether it should be.

Coverage D: Loss of Use / Additional Living Expenses

Coverage D applies when your home becomes uninhabitable after a covered loss. It pays for temporary housing, meals above your normal food budget, laundry, storage, and other reasonable costs incurred while your home is being repaired or rebuilt.

This coverage has a limit, and it matters more than most homeowners realize. If your home sustains major damage and repairs take six months or more, Coverage D is what keeps you housed and functional during that time. Understand your limit and how it is structured. Some policies express it as a dollar limit; others express it as a time limit or percentage of Coverage A.

Your Deductibles

Your declarations page will show one or more deductibles. This is the amount you pay out of pocket before insurance covers the rest of a claim.

Most policies carry an all-other-perils (AOP) deductible, which applies to most covered losses. This is often a flat dollar amount such as $1,000 or $2,500. You can read a more detailed breakdown of how this deductible works in our article What Does AOP Deductible Mean and How Does It Affect Your Claim?

Many policies in areas prone to wind, hail, or hurricanes carry a separate deductible for those perils specifically. This deductible is frequently expressed as a percentage of your Coverage A limit rather than a flat dollar amount. A 2% wind deductible on a $350,000 Coverage A limit means $7,000 out of pocket on a wind or hail claim before insurance pays anything. If you live in a storm-prone state and have not confirmed this number recently, now is the time.

Knowing both deductibles before a storm arrives matters. It affects how you evaluate whether to file a claim, and it affects your financial planning for recovery.

Coverage E and F: Liability and Medical Payments

Coverage E is personal liability coverage. If someone is injured on your property or you are found legally responsible for damage to another person's property, Coverage E is what responds. Review this limit and consider whether it is adequate given your assets and circumstances.

Coverage F is medical payments to others. This is a smaller, no-fault coverage that pays for minor medical expenses if a guest is injured on your property, regardless of whether you are legally liable. It is typically a much smaller limit than Coverage E.

While these coverages are less directly connected to a property damage claim, they appear on your dec page and are worth understanding.

Endorsements Listed on the Declarations Page

Near the bottom of your declarations page, or sometimes on a second page, you will typically find a list of endorsements attached to your policy. An endorsement is a modification to your standard policy terms. It may add coverage, restrict it, or change how something is settled.

Common endorsements include water backup and sump pump overflow coverage, scheduled personal property for specific high-value items, equipment breakdown coverage, extended replacement cost or guaranteed replacement cost provisions, and roof-specific settlement modifications.

If an endorsement is listed, pull that document from your policy package and read it. Endorsements can meaningfully change what is and is not covered, and they are often overlooked until a claim is underway. An endorsement that limits your roof to ACV settlement, for example, could significantly reduce a payout you expected to receive at replacement cost.

What to Do With This Information Right Now

Pull your current declarations page out of your files or log into your insurer's portal to access it. Work through each of the following:

  • Confirm your policy period is current and active.
  • Verify the named insured and property address are accurate.
  • Review your Coverage A limit and assess whether it reflects today's rebuild costs for your home.
  • Confirm whether your personal property settles at ACV or replacement cost.
  • Identify all deductibles, including any separate wind or hail deductible, and note the dollar amount each represents.
  • List every endorsement shown and locate those documents in your policy package.
  • Note your Coverage D limit and understand how it is structured.

This review takes less than twenty minutes and gives you a complete picture of where your coverage stands before damage happens. The policyholders who navigate claims most effectively are almost always the ones who understood their policy before they needed it.

A Note on What the Dec Page Does Not Tell You

The declarations page does not list your exclusions. It does not explain the conditions you must meet to receive a claim payment. It does not describe how depreciation is calculated or what documentation is required to recover it. All of that lives in the policy form itself.

The dec page is your starting point, not your finish line. Once you understand your coverage limits and deductibles, the next step is reviewing the loss settlement provisions and exclusions in the full policy so there are no surprises at claim time.

If you are currently in the middle of a claim and trying to understand what your policy covers, our article How to Document Property Damage for Your Insurance Claim is a useful next read.

Final Thought

Your declarations page is one page. It takes less time to read than most articles you will encounter today. But the numbers on it govern what happens financially if your home is damaged, and most homeowners have never looked at it carefully.

A renewal notice is not just a premium amount. It is your annual invitation to confirm that your coverage still fits your home and your life.

The best time to understand your policy is before you need it. The second best time is right now.
