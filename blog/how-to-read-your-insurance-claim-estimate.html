How to Read Your Insurance Claim Estimate Line by Line

Most policyholders look at the bottom number and stop there. The line items above it tell a far more important story about what your insurer is, and is not, accounting for.

After an adjuster inspects your property, you will eventually receive an estimate. It may arrive as a multi-page document full of unfamiliar abbreviations, columns of numbers, and line items written in trade language most homeowners have never encountered.

Many policyholders flip to the last page, look at the total, and assume that number represents a fair accounting of their damage. It may, or it may not. The only way to know is to understand what you are actually looking at.

This article walks through how insurance estimates are structured, what the most important terms mean, and what to look for when reviewing your own estimate before any repair decisions are made.

What Kind of Estimate Will You Receive?

The most common format used by insurance companies and their adjusters is produced by a software platform called Xactimate. It is an industry-standard estimating tool that generates line-item repair and replacement cost estimates based on standardized pricing databases for labor and materials, typically organized by geographic region.

Not every insurer uses Xactimate, and some use proprietary or alternative platforms. However, Xactimate is prevalent enough that understanding its format gives you a strong foundation for reading almost any estimate you receive. The structure, terminology, and column layout translate across most formats.

Whether your estimate comes from Xactimate or another system, the core components are the same: a list of line items describing work to be done, quantities, unit pricing, and totals, organized by room or trade category, followed by a summary section that applies depreciation, deductibles, and prior payments to arrive at a net amount owed.

The Header: Confirm the Basics First

Before reviewing a single line item, check the header information on the first page of the estimate. This section typically includes your name, claim number, policy number, date of loss, and the property address.

Confirm all of it is accurate. A wrong date of loss can affect what is covered. A wrong address creates a documentation mismatch. A claim number that does not match your records is worth flagging immediately. These details may seem administrative, but they matter if questions arise later.

The Scope of Loss: What the Estimate Says Was Damaged

The body of the estimate is often called the scope of loss. This is the adjuster's description of what was damaged and what work they have determined is needed to repair or replace it. In Xactimate, this is organized by room, area, or trade, such as exterior, roofing, kitchen, or living room.

Read through every section. For each area of your home that sustained damage, ask yourself whether the items listed accurately reflect what you saw when you documented the damage. Common things to look for include:

  • Rooms or areas that experienced damage but do not appear in the estimate at all.
  • Line items that list a smaller quantity than the actual affected area. For example, an estimate that accounts for 200 square feet of flooring when the affected room is 300 square feet.
  • Items listed as repair when replacement may be more appropriate based on the extent of damage.
  • Related or connected damage that was not included. For example, water damage to a ceiling may involve not only drywall but also insulation, paint, and potentially structural components depending on severity.
  • Code upgrade requirements. Some repairs must meet current building codes even if the original installation did not. Costs associated with bringing repaired areas into code compliance are sometimes omitted from initial estimates.

This is where your own documentation becomes critical. If you photographed the damage thoroughly before cleanup began, you have a reference point to compare against what appears in the estimate. Our article How to Document Property Damage for Your Insurance Claim covers what to capture and preserve so your documentation can support exactly this kind of review.

Line Item Columns: What Each Number Represents

Each line item in an Xactimate estimate typically includes several columns. Understanding what each column represents helps you evaluate whether the pricing and quantities are reasonable.

Description

The description column names the specific task or material. These descriptions are often abbreviated or written in trade language. Common examples include items like "R&R" which stands for remove and replace, "Detach and reset" which means removing an item to perform work behind it and then reinstalling it, or material descriptions like "LVP flooring" for luxury vinyl plank. If a description is unclear, it is reasonable to ask your adjuster to explain what that line item covers.

Quantity and Unit

This column shows how much of the item is being estimated. It may be expressed in square feet, linear feet, square yards, hours, or individual units. Measure the affected areas yourself and compare them to the quantities listed. Discrepancies in quantity are one of the most common sources of estimate disputes.

Unit Price

The unit price is what Xactimate has assigned for that specific task or material in your region. Xactimate prices are updated periodically and are intended to reflect local market rates. However, actual contractor pricing in your area may differ, particularly in the current environment of elevated material and labor costs. If contractor quotes you obtain are significantly higher than what appears on the estimate, that difference can be the basis for a supplement request.

RCV (Replacement Cost Value)

The RCV column shows the full estimated cost to repair or replace that item with similar materials, without any deduction for depreciation. This is the gross cost before any withholding is applied.

Depreciation

The depreciation column shows the amount withheld from each line item based on age, condition, and useful life. Depreciation is applied differently to different items. Roofing, flooring, and appliances typically carry higher depreciation than structural framing or concrete. The depreciation amount is either recoverable or non-recoverable depending on your policy's settlement method. Understanding which applies to your policy is essential before interpreting these numbers. You can read more about how that distinction works in our article Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost: What Is the Difference?

ACV (Actual Cash Value)

The ACV column shows the net value after depreciation has been applied. On an RCV policy, this is typically the amount of the initial payment. The difference between RCV and ACV is the recoverable depreciation, which may be reimbursed after qualifying repairs are completed and documented per your policy's requirements.

The Summary Page: Where Everything Is Calculated

At the end of the estimate, there is a summary section that applies adjustments to the line item totals. This is where the final payment amount is calculated. Read through each line carefully.

Replacement Cost Value Total

This is the sum of all RCV figures across every line item in the estimate. It represents the gross estimated cost to complete all listed repairs.

Less Depreciation

This line subtracts the total depreciation withheld across all line items. The resulting figure is the ACV total for the entire estimate.

Less Deductible

Your applicable deductible is subtracted here. Confirm that the correct deductible is being applied. If your loss was caused by wind or hail and your policy carries a separate wind or hail deductible, verify that the correct figure appears. If a flat all-other-perils deductible is being applied when a percentage-based wind deductible should be used, or vice versa, that is worth raising with your adjuster.

Net Claim / ACV Payment

This is the initial payment amount the insurer is releasing based on the estimate. On an RCV policy, this is typically the ACV total minus the deductible. On an ACV policy, this may represent the final payment for that portion of the claim.

Prior Payments

If any payments have already been issued on the claim, such as an advance or emergency mitigation payment, they may be listed here and subtracted from the net amount. Confirm these figures match what you have actually received.

Overhead and Profit: An Important Line Item to Understand

One of the most frequently debated items in insurance estimates is overhead and profit, commonly abbreviated as O&P. This line item represents the general contractor's cost of managing a repair project, including business operating costs and a reasonable profit margin.

O&P is generally considered appropriate when the scope of work requires coordinating multiple subcontractors or trades, which is common in significant losses involving roofing, siding, interior repairs, and related work performed together. It is not always included in initial estimates, and its absence does not necessarily mean it is unavailable. If you are working with a general contractor and O&P is not reflected in your estimate, it is a reasonable item to raise when reviewing the estimate with your adjuster or when submitting a supplement.

What Is Commonly Missing From Initial Estimates

Initial estimates are based on what an adjuster observed and documented during the inspection. They are not always complete. Some of the most frequently missing or underrepresented items include:

  • Damage to areas that were not fully accessible during the inspection, such as attic spaces, wall cavities, or areas beneath flooring.
  • Code upgrade requirements, also called code and ordinance items, which reflect the cost of bringing repaired work into compliance with current building codes.
  • Permits required for the repair work.
  • Temporary protection measures such as tarping, board-up, or temporary fencing that may have been necessary to prevent further damage.
  • Matching requirements. In some cases, repairing a portion of a surface, such as siding or flooring, requires replacing a larger area to achieve a reasonable match. Whether and how matching costs are covered varies by policy and state.
  • Items discovered after initial inspection during the course of repairs. These are legitimate candidates for a supplement request once identified and documented.

What to Do After Reviewing the Estimate

Once you have read through the estimate carefully, compare it against your own damage documentation and any contractor estimates you have obtained. Note any discrepancies, missing items, or questions about specific line items.

If you find items that appear to be missing or underpriced, a supplement request is the appropriate path. A supplement is a formal, documented request to your insurer for additional coverage based on items not captured in the original estimate. It is not a dispute or a confrontation. It is the standard mechanism for addressing gaps between what was initially estimated and what the repairs actually require.

Our article How to Request a Supplement on Your Insurance Claim covers how to approach that process, what to include, and how to document your position clearly.

If the estimate reflects items that were denied entirely rather than simply underestimated, and you believe those items should be covered under your policy, that is a different situation that may involve a formal appeal. Our article My Insurance Claim Was Denied. What Do I Do Next? addresses that process.

A Note on Timing

If you have not yet received your estimate and the adjuster inspection is upcoming, this article still applies. Understanding the structure of an estimate before the inspection helps you know what to ask about, what to point out during the walkthrough, and what to document on your own so you have a reference when the estimate arrives.

If you have already received an estimate and repairs are underway, it is not too late to review it carefully. As long as your claim is open, discrepancies and omissions can still be addressed through the supplement process.

Final Thought

An insurance estimate is not a final verdict. It is a document prepared by one party, based on one inspection, at one point in time. It reflects what the adjuster observed and what the estimating system produced. That does not make it wrong, but it does make it worth reviewing.

Policyholders who take the time to read their estimates line by line, compare them to their own documentation, and ask questions when something does not align are in a significantly stronger position throughout the claims process.

You do not need to be an expert to read your estimate. You need to be willing to read it at all.
